# Chapter 3: The Three Currencies — What Runs Through You

---

**3:17 p.m. on a Tuesday.**

The home office is too small for the three monitors, the ergonomic chair, the half-drunk coffee gone cold hours ago. The fluorescent light hums like a trapped insect. Your fingers hover over the keyboard, but the words won’t come—not because you don’t know what to say, but because your hands are shaking, just slightly, like you’ve had too much caffeine. You haven’t. You had one cup at 7:30 a.m.

Your shoulders are up by your ears. You didn’t put them there. Your jaw is clenched so hard it aches, and when you try to relax it, your teeth grind together anyway. Your breath is shallow, caught in the upper part of your chest, like you’re bracing for something. The dog whines at the door. The laundry’s still in the machine. The email you’ve been staring at for twenty minutes is still blank.

You just hung up from the third back-to-back Zoom meeting. The last one was with Priya, who pushed back on every idea you floated. You meant to stay calm. You *tried*. But by the end, your voice had an edge you didn’t mean to use, and now you’re replaying the last exchange in your head—not the content, just the tone—hers, then yours, then hers again, like a loop of static.

You feel wired. Like if someone asked you one more thing, you might jump out of your skin. But also exhausted. Like if someone asked you to do one more thing, you might snap.

You take a sip of the room-temperature water. The glass feels heavy in your hand. You exhale. It comes out shorter than you meant. You try again, longer this time, and something in your chest eases—just a notch, but enough to notice. The dog’s tail thumps against the floor.

You haven’t moved from this chair since 8:45 a.m., except to refill your water and use the bathroom. You close your eyes for three seconds. When you open them, the blank email draft is still there. But the room feels a little less close to your face.

---

**8:42 a.m. on a Saturday.**

The baby monitor crackles with the sound of your eight-week-old stirring. You’re already awake—you’ve been awake since 4:17 a.m., when the reflux flared and you spent the next hour rocking, burping, wiping, trying to soothe a tiny body that didn’t want to be soothed. Your partner is asleep in the guest room, catching up after their night shift. You’re alone with the baby, the quiet house, and the weight of another day that hasn’t even started yet.

You stand up from the couch, where you’ve been sitting in the dark, scrolling mindlessly on your phone. Your limbs feel heavy, like they’re moving through water. Your eyes burn—not from tears, but from something deeper, grittier, like sand behind your eyelids. You haven’t showered in two days. You can’t remember the last time you ate a meal that wasn’t a granola bar or cold leftovers eaten standing up.

The baby starts to fuss in earnest. You pick them up, and their tiny body curls into yours, warm and fragile. You feel a surge of love—real, deep, overwhelming—but it’s followed immediately by a wave of exhaustion so profound it steals your breath. You sit down in the rocking chair, and your body feels like it’s made of lead. Your chest feels flat, like there’s nothing left inside. No spark. No drive. Just the mechanical act of keeping this tiny human alive.

You rock. The baby settles. You stare at the wall, and for a moment, you don’t even know what you’re supposed to be doing next. The to-do list in your head is a fog. You can’t remember if you’re supposed to be making breakfast, or showering, or responding to the text from your mom that’s been sitting unread since yesterday.

You feel like you’re moving through molasses, and every small task feels like climbing a mountain. You close your eyes. You don’t mean to drift off, but you do, just for a moment. When you open them, the baby is asleep in your arms, and your neck is stiff from the awkward angle. You don’t dare move. You just sit there, staring at the wall, feeling the weight of your own body, the weight of the baby, the weight of the day ahead.

You feel like you’re running on fumes, and the tank is empty.

---

**Two weeks after the flu.**

You’re standing in the kitchen, staring into the fridge. You’re supposed to be making dinner, but your body feels like it’s made of lead. Your limbs are heavy. Your mind is sluggish. You can’t remember the last time you felt *well*. The flu knocked you out for ten days, and now, two weeks later, you’re still not back to normal.

You keep telling yourself you’re fine, that you just need to push through, but your body isn’t listening. You reach for the yogurt, but your hand trembles slightly. You’re not hungry, but you know you should eat something. You take a bite, and it tastes like nothing. You force yourself to swallow.

Your chest feels tight, like there’s a band wrapped around it. Your breath is shallow, like you’re not getting enough air. You sit down at the table, and your body feels like it’s sinking into the chair. You try to think about the work you’re supposed to be doing. The emails you need to answer. The project that’s due next week. But your mind feels foggy, like you’re thinking through a thick haze. You can’t focus. You can’t summon the energy to care.

You feel like you’re running on empty, and there’s nothing left in the tank. You close your eyes. You don’t mean to drift off, but you do, just for a moment. When you open them, the yogurt is still half-eaten in front of you, and the kitchen is quiet.

You feel like you’re moving through water, and every small task feels like an impossible effort. You feel like you’re overheating, like your body is running too hot, and there’s no way to cool it down.

---

You’ve felt these things before. Not just today. Not just this week. You’ve felt them in different forms, in different parts of your life—the wired, jittery feeling after too many meetings; the flat, heavy exhaustion after weeks of sleepless nights; the foggy, sluggish mind after illness; the tight, clenched chest after an argument; the shaky, unstable energy after skipping meals.

You’ve called them different things—stress, exhaustion, burnout, anxiety, overwhelm—but they’re all variations of the same thing. They’re the signals your system sends when something is off. When it’s running too hot, or too cold. When it’s holding too much, or not enough. When it’s empty, or unstable, or overheating.

And here’s the thing: these signals aren’t random. They’re not just moods. They’re data. They’re the way your system tells you what it needs, what it’s costing you, and what would actually restore it.

You run on three currencies. Three things you already feel, every day, in your body.

**Charge. Pressure. Fuel.**

---

## Charge: How Switched-On You Are

**Too much charge:**
You’re buzzing. Jittery. Can’t sit still. Can’t switch off. Everything feels urgent, like if you don’t act *now*, something terrible will happen. Your fingers tap on the table. Your jaw clenches. Your mind races ahead of your words. Your heart feels like it’s going to burst out of your chest. You feel like you could jump out of your skin if someone asked you one more thing.

This is over-charge. Too much current running through you. It’s what happens after too much caffeine, too many meetings, too much stimulation with no break. It’s what happens when your nervous system is stuck in performance mode, and there’s no off switch.

*What it feels like in the body:*
- Fingers tapping, legs bouncing, restless hands
- Jaw clenched, teeth grinding
- Mind racing, thoughts jumping from one thing to the next
- Heart pounding, breath shallow
- Shoulders up by your ears, like you’re bracing for impact
- Feeling like you’re about to snap, or explode, or crawl out of your skin

*What restores it:*
- **Movement.** A fast walk, stairs, dancing in your kitchen—anything that makes you breathe hard for ten minutes.
- **Breath.** Specifically, breathing out longer than you breathe in. Try it now: breathe in for four counts, out for six. Do it six times. Notice how your shoulders drop, just a little.
- **Cold.** Splash your face with cold water. Step outside without a jacket. Hold an ice cube in your hand. Cold is a direct line to the switch that turns performance mode off.
- **Discharge.** Shake it out—literally. Hands, arms, whole body. Thirty seconds. It looks silly. It works. Animals do it after a fright for a reason.

**Too little charge:**
You’re numb. Blank. Frozen. Gone quiet inside. Your limbs feel heavy. Your voice has no spark. Your mind feels slow, like it’s moving through molasses. You feel like you’re moving through water, and every small task feels like an impossible effort. You’re not just tired—you’re shut down.

This is under-charge. The circuit’s tripped. It’s what happens after too much stress, too little sleep, too much emotional weight with no release. It’s what happens when your system hits its own breaker to protect itself.

*What it feels like in the body:*
- Heavy limbs, slow blinking
- No spark in your voice, no energy in your movements
- Mind foggy, thoughts sluggish
- Breath shallow, like you’re not getting enough air
- Feeling like you’re moving through molasses, or water, or a dream

*What restores it:*
- **Gentle re-activation.** Small movement—stretching, walking slowly, shaking out your hands.
- **Cold splash on your face.** Lie down with your eyes closed for five minutes. Sip water slowly.
- **Connection.** A hug, a real conversation, eye contact with someone who steadies you.
- **Safety.** Under-charge often comes from feeling unsafe. Do one small thing that tells your system it’s okay to come back online—a warm drink, a cozy blanket, a familiar song.

## Pressure: What You’re Holding

**Too much pressure:**
You’re holding it together with your teeth. Your chest feels tight, like there’s a band wrapped around it. Your jaw is clenched. Your shoulders are up by your ears. Your breath is shallow, caught in the upper part of your chest. You feel like you’re carrying a physical weight, and it’s pressing down on you, making everything harder.

This is too much pressure. Running against permanent resistance. It’s what happens when you’re holding too much—too many tasks, too many emotions, too many unresolved things—and there’s no release. It’s what happens when your system is clogged, and the waste isn’t clearing.

*What it feels like in the body:*
- Tight chest, like a band is wrapped around it
- Clenched jaw, sore to touch
- Shoulders up by your ears, like you’re bracing for impact
- Shallow breathing, like you’re not getting enough air
- Trouble sleeping, because the pressure won’t let you relax
- A physical weight, like something heavy is pressing down on you

*What restores it:*
- **Release.** Move it—walk fast, dance, shake it out. Ten minutes of hard movement can shift what hours of thinking can’t.
- **Breath.** Long exhales, like you’re sighing out the pressure. Try it now: breathe in for four counts, out for eight. Notice how your chest loosens, just a little.
- **Words.** Put the pressure into words—write it down, or tell someone you trust. The point is *out*, not *held*.
- **Rest.** Not sleep—just lying down with your eyes closed, letting your body feel the support beneath it.

**Poor flow:**
You’re foggy. Sluggish. Swampy. Stuck. Your mind feels like it’s moving through molasses. Your body feels heavy. Your thoughts are slow, like they’re wading through thick air. You feel like you’re running against resistance, and nothing is moving the way it should.

This is poor flow. The waste isn’t clearing. It’s what happens when you’re not discharging the pressure regularly—when you’re holding it in, or when your system is clogged. It’s what happens when you’re not sleeping enough, or moving enough, or letting things drain the way they need to.

*What it feels like in the body:*
- Foggy head, sluggish body
- Dread about the next thing, like it’s going to be too much
- Breath feels stuck, like you’re not getting enough air
- Feeling like you’re moving through molasses, or water, or a dream
- A swampy, stuck feeling, like nothing is moving the way it should

*What restores it:*
- **Discharge.** Move hard for ten minutes—walk, run, dance, shake it out. Let the pressure move.
- **Hydrate.** Drink a glass of water slowly, noticing how it feels in your body.
- **Sleep.** Poor flow is often a sign that your system isn’t getting enough rest to clear the waste. Prioritize sleep, even if it means saying no to something else.
- **Words.** Put the pressure into words—write it down, or tell someone you trust. The point is *out*, not *held*.

## Fuel: How Much You’ve Got in the Tank

**Empty:**
You’ve got nothing left. Even small things feel impossible. Your limbs feel heavy. Your mind feels foggy. Your chest feels flat, like there’s no spark left inside. You feel like you’re running on fumes, and the tank is empty. You’re not just tired—you’re depleted.

This is an empty tank. It’s what happens after too much output with not enough input—too little sleep, too little food, too little rest. It’s what happens when you’ve been pushing a system that’s already empty, and there’s nothing left to give.

*What it feels like in the body:*
- No energy, even for small things
- Heavy limbs, slow blinking
- No appetite, or eating without tasting
- Breath shallow, like you’re not getting enough air
- Chest feels flat, like there’s no spark left inside

*What restores it:*
- **Real rest.** Not scrolling, not watching TV—actual rest. Lie down with your eyes closed. Take a nap. Sit quietly for ten minutes.
- **Real food.** Not snacks, not caffeine—actual nourishment. Protein, complex carbs, something that will steady your blood sugar.
- **Real recovery.** Not pushing through—actually stopping. Saying no to one more thing. Taking something off the pile.

**Unstable:**
You’re fine, then suddenly not. You’re steady, then shaky. You’re focused, then foggy. You’re full of energy, then crashing. You’re running on a sugar spike-and-crash, or a caffeine high that fades into exhaustion. You feel like you’re on a rollercoaster, and there’s no steady ground.

This is unstable fuel. It’s what happens when your energy is coming from quick fixes—caffeine, sugar, adrenaline—instead of real nourishment. It’s what happens when you’re not eating regularly, or not sleeping enough, or not giving your body what it needs to run steadily.

*What it feels like in the body:*
- Hands shaky, irritable
- Mind foggy, then suddenly racing
- Energy spikes, then crashes
- Feeling fine, then suddenly exhausted
- Hunger pangs, then sudden irritability

*What restores it:*
- Eat protein before events. Take breaks to sit quietly. Hydrate well.
- Prioritize steady meals—protein + complex carbs—over quick fixes.
- Notice the pattern. When do you crash? What were you doing before? What would steady you?

**Overheating:**
You’ve been running too hot for too long. You’re exhausted, but sleep doesn’t touch it. You’re flat, but caffeine doesn’t help. You’re running on empty, and there’s nothing left in the tank. You feel like you’re close to burnout, and there’s no way to cool down.

This is overheating. It’s what happens after too much for too long—too many commitments, too much stress, too little rest. It’s what happens when you’ve been pushing a system that’s already empty, and there’s no way to refill it without stopping.

*What it feels like in the body:*
- Exhaustion sleep doesn’t touch
- Flatness, creeping cynicism
- Not caring about things that used to matter
- Running on empty with nothing left
- Feeling like you’re close to burnout

*What restores it:*
- **Stop adding.** No new commitments, no new goals. A system in the red doesn’t get given more.
- **Take something off the pile.** One real thing, dropped or delayed. This is the hard part. Do it anyway.
- **Protect the basics fiercely**—sleep, food, one gentle bit of movement—and let everything else be “good enough” for now.

## Putting It Together

In any moment when you feel “off,” ask: *What currency is off?*

- Am I over-charged? (buzzing, jittery, can’t sit still)
- Am I under-charged? (numb, blank, gone quiet)
- Am I holding too much pressure? (tight chest, clenched jaw, a weight on my chest)
- Is the pressure stuck? (foggy, sluggish, swampy)
- Is my fuel empty? (nothing left, even small things feel impossible)
- Is my fuel unstable? (spike-and-crash, shaky, irritable)
- Am I overheating? (too much for too long, close to burnout)

Then try the thing that restores it. Not the thing you think you *should* do—the thing your body is actually asking for.

**Lena, 29, teacher.**

It’s Thursday afternoon, and Lena is standing in her classroom after the last bell, staring at the pile of ungraded papers on her desk. Her shoulders are up by her ears. Her jaw is clenched. Her breath is shallow, caught in the upper part of her chest. She feels wired, like if someone asked her one more thing, she might snap.

She’s been here before. Every Thursday, after a week of back-to-back classes, meetings, and parent emails, she feels like this—wired, exhausted, and on edge. She calls it “teacher brain.” She tells herself she just needs to push through, get the papers graded, and then she can relax.

But today, she notices something different. She notices the currency that’s off. She’s over-charged—buzzing, jittery, can’t sit still. And she knows what restores it: movement. Breath. Cold.

She puts the papers in her bag. She walks out of the classroom, down the hall, and out the side door of the school. She doesn’t have her coat, but she doesn’t care. She walks fast, arms swinging, breath coming hard. She walks for ten minutes, until her hands stop shaking and her shoulders drop.

Then she sits on a bench, closes her eyes, and breathes—long exhales, out for six counts, in for four. When she opens her eyes, the world feels a little less close to her face. The papers are still waiting, but they don’t feel like an impossible task anymore. She feels steadier. More capable. Like she can actually do this.

## The Three Currencies — What Runs Through You

You run on three things you already feel—**charge**, **pressure**, and **fuel**. They’re the electricity, the flow, and the energy in the tank. Whatever layer a problem sits on, it’s always costing you something in one of these three.

So there are two ways to read yourself:

- **Which currency?** — *what it feels like, and what restores it*
- **Which layer?** — *where it lives* (we’ll get to that in the next chapters)

For now, just learn to spot the currency. Because once you can name what’s off, you can do something about it.

---
**Notice this in you.**

**Notice** — Today, notice one moment when you felt “off”—not good or bad, just different from your usual. Where in your body did you feel it? Was it in your chest? Your jaw? Your limbs? Your mind?

**Trace** — Ask: am I over-charged (wired, jittery, can’t sit still)? under-charged (flat, numb, gone quiet)? holding too much pressure (tight, clenched, a weight on my chest)? running low on fuel (empty, shaky, nothing left)? Be specific about the feeling in your body. Name the currency that’s most off.

**Try** — Pick the currency that’s most off and do one small thing to shift it:

- *Over-charge*: 3–5 long exhales (Tool 1) or 10 minutes of movement (walk, stretch, shake it out).
- *Under-charge*: 5 minutes of gentle re-activation (cold splash on face, lie down with eyes closed, sip water slowly).
- *Too much pressure*: 3 minutes of discharge (Tool 3)—move hard for 10 minutes, shake it out, or put the pressure into words with someone you trust.
- *Fuel low/unstable*: Eat one real meal (protein + complex carbs), hydrate well, or take 20 minutes to lie down and rest without guilt (Tool 5).

**Watch** — Notice what shifts in the next hour or day. Did the feeling change? Did your capacity change? Did you handle something better? Or did nothing shift—and if so, what does that tell you?

**Adjust** — If it helped, keep it and add nothing yet. If it didn’t, the real dip might be elsewhere—you’ve just ruled one thing out. That’s progress, not failure.

*One line for the picture of yourself: “The currency I most often run low on is _______. What restores it is _______.”*

---
That moment when you noticed the currency that was off—and tried the thing that restored it—is the first step toward reading yourself like a system.
