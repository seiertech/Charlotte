# Chapter 3: Wiring — The Part of You That Moves First

Callum gets home at twenty to nine.

He'd said seven. He knows he said seven. The review overran, then everyone went for a drink, and he meant to text but his phone was in his bag and then it was eight and then it was too late to text without it sounding worse.

His partner is on the sofa, not angry, just tired. "You said you'd be back by seven."

"Don't *start*."

It's out before he's even put his keys down. There's that half-second where his voice slips its gear and fires on its own, the words already loose before he meant them. He watches it land. He watches the face across the room close a little.

And the worst part is, he doesn't even disagree. He *was* late. He *should* have texted. If someone had asked him calmly an hour ago, he'd have said all of that himself. But nobody asked him calmly an hour ago. Someone said one mild sentence, and something in him decided it was an attack before the rest of him had arrived.

Callum walks to the window and stands there with his stomach dropping. Somewhere behind the shame is a kitchen in Glasgow, loud and crowded, where you spoke first and fastest or you didn't get heard at all.

He learned that before he could tie his shoes. His wiring never unlearned it.

---

## The automatic part of you

This is your **Wiring**: the automatic operating system running beneath your awareness. Your reactions. Your threat responses. The flinch, the snap, the freeze, the tears that arrive before you know why. The deep patterns, some of them set very early, that fire before conscious thought has even shown up.

You've felt it a thousand times. The tight jaw before you've decided to speak. The stomach drop before you've named the feeling. The voice that comes out too loud before you've chosen the words. That isn't your body acting up, and it isn't a decision. It's your wiring, already in gear before you've sat down in the driver's seat.

There's a good reason it's built this way. The brain has a fast route and a slow route. The fast route catches signs of danger and fires a response in a fraction of a second, before the slower, thinking part of you has finished working out what's going on.^5^ If a car swerves towards you, that speed saves your life. You jump back, and only afterwards do you realise what happened.

The trouble is that the fast route doesn't only fire for cars. It fires for tones of voice. For a certain kind of silence. For a name in your inbox, a door closing too hard, a mild sentence on a sofa at twenty to nine. It learned what danger looked like a long time ago, and it's still using the old pictures.

---

## Your feelings are alarms, not verdicts

Here's a way of seeing your emotions that takes a lot of the shame out of them.

- **Fear** isn't weakness. It's a smoke alarm, ringing in your wiring first.
- **Anger** isn't a character flaw. It's an alert that says *someone has crossed a line.*
- **Sadness** isn't a mood you've chosen. It's the system adjusting after a loss, saying *this mattered.*
- **Worry** isn't a personality. It's your wiring running *what if* on a future that hasn't happened, trying to prepare you for threats that may never arrive.

None of these alarms are the problem. They're the system doing its job. The problem is when an alarm is calibrated for a different house, a different decade, a different you, and it goes off at full volume in a room that is actually safe.

That's not something to be ashamed of. It's something to update.

---

## Sleep is where the wiring gets maintained

One more thing about this layer, because it connects straight back to the last chapter.

Sleep isn't only rest for the body. It's maintenance for the wiring. Overnight, the brain files the day away, settles what was stirred up, and resets the alarm system for the morning.^6^ Skip it, and the alarms get louder. After one night without sleep, the brain's threat centre reacts dramatically more strongly to upsetting things.^2^

So on the mornings after a bad night, it isn't your imagination. Everything really does feel more like an attack. Your wiring is running without its nightly service.

This is why the order of the layers matters. Callum's wiring was always going to be fast. But on four hours' sleep, after a long review, on an empty stomach, it wasn't just fast. It was hair-trigger.

## How to tell it's the Wiring

There's one simple question for this layer:

**Did this happen before I had time to think? Was it already in motion before I chose it?**

If yes, you're very likely looking at Wiring.

It gets trickier when a reaction comes with a sentence attached. A feeling like *I'm not enough* could live in your Wiring, as an old alarm set early that fires instantly as a body-state with no words. Or it could live in your Story, as a narrative you tell yourself, a sentence you can actually hear. Two questions help you tell them apart:

1. **How fast did it arrive?** If it was there instantly, before any thought, as a feeling in your body, that points to Wiring.
2. **Can you hear a sentence?** If there's a clear line of narration, *I'm not enough*, *they'll find me out*, then Story is involved.

Very often it's both: the wiring fires first, and the story narrates it a moment later. *You walk into a room of strangers and instantly feel small. Then the sentence arrives: see, you don't belong here.* When that happens, work with the wiring first. The story will keep regenerating for as long as the alarm keeps feeding it.

---

## What it gets mistaken for

**"That's just how I am."** It feels like personality, but it's wiring: worn in, not fixed. The quick temper, the freeze in a conflict, the urge to please: these are patterns, and patterns can be updated.

**"I've got a bad habit of snapping."** The snap looks like a habit, but you can't break it with habit tools, because it fires before the part of you that makes choices has arrived. Willpower turns up too late.

**"I'm just tired."** Sometimes. But if you've slept, eaten and rested and the alarm still fires, the tiredness isn't the cause. Something in the wiring is.

---

## What it's costing you

The three currencies show up in the wiring like this.

**Charge** is the big one. An alarm going off is a spike in Charge: heart up, heat in the face, the urge to fight, run or shut the door. Wiring can also crash the other way, into freeze: blank, numb, gone quiet, unable to find a single word.

**Pressure** builds when a reaction is caught halfway and swallowed. The anger you didn't say. The tears you held back in the meeting. It doesn't disappear. It sits in the jaw, the chest, the throat, and it waits.

**Fuel** is the buffer. When your tank is full, a small trigger produces a small reaction, because you have room to absorb it. When it's empty, every small trigger produces a big reaction, because there's no buffer left at all.

## How it shows up

**Elena** walks into the school's board meeting and, before anyone has said a word, feels herself shrink. Her chest tightens; her face gets warm. There's no sentence yet. Just a wordless, instant sense of being smaller than everyone in the room. That's Wiring. The sentence, *you're about to be found out*, arrives thirty seconds later, narrating what the alarm already decided.

**Maren** is at home on a rare day off when the microwave beeps, and she's on her feet, heart pounding, before she knows why. Two years of monitors and alarms have taught her body that a beep means someone might be dying. Her wiring doesn't know she's in her own kitchen.

**Grace** is asked, in a regional meeting, "What do you think, Grace?" and her mind goes completely white. She has opinions. She has fifteen years of opinions. But the moment the spotlight lands, something in her freezes, and she hears herself say, "I'm happy with whatever everyone decides."

Three different alarms. All of them faster than thought.

## The tools for this layer

Wiring works too fast to argue with. You can't talk an alarm out of ringing. But you can do two things that reach it directly. You'll find both in full in Chapter 8.

**The Long Exhale.** Breathe in through your nose for a count of four. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of six or more. The out-breath has to be longer than the in-breath; that's the whole trick. Do it about six times, roughly a minute. A long, slow out-breath is one of the clearest signals you can send your nervous system that the danger has passed, and slow breathing reliably helps people settle.^7^

**Name It to Tame It.** Put the feeling into the plainest words you can, silently or out loud: *This is anger. This is fear. I feel small and I want to hide.* It sounds too simple to work. But putting a feeling into words measurably calms the brain's alarm response,^8^ moving the feeling from the fast, wordless part of you to the slower part that can hold it. You're still feeling it. But now you're *beside* it, not *inside* it.

---

## Try this: the one-breath pause

**Notice.** Look for a moment today when you reacted *before* you thought: a flinch, a snap, a freeze, a sudden tightness in your throat. Not the big dramatic moments. The quiet ones.

**Trace.** What set it off? A tone of voice, a sudden movement, a word, a silence? Name the trigger without judging it. Does it feel older than the moment, like a pattern worn in long ago?

**Try.** Next time you feel that automatic reaction coming, take *one* long breath out before you respond. Let the reaction arrive. Name it silently: *That's my wiring.* Then choose what to do.

**Watch.** Did the reaction still happen? Did the response feel any different? Was the Charge a little lower afterwards?

**Adjust.** If the pause helped, keep it. If not, ask what the real trigger was. Was it something older than the moment? No need to fix it yet. Just notice.

## When it's bigger than an everyday alarm

Some wiring has been shaped by things that were genuinely dangerous: violence, loss, long periods of fear. If your alarm system is still braced for something that happened years ago, that isn't weakness. It's a nervous system that never got a clear all-clear. That kind of wiring deserves patient, skilled help, from someone trained to work with it, not a breathing exercise and a stiff upper lip.

Some wiring is simply different. Many neurodivergent people process light, sound or touch more intensely, so a flickering bulb or a crowded room can set off a real alarm. That isn't something broken to be fixed. It's a different wiring to be understood and accommodated.

And if panic comes from nowhere, comes often, or never seems to switch off, that's worth taking to a doctor. A tool can take the top off a moment. It isn't meant to carry a pattern.

## What Callum did next

Callum stood at the window for a long minute. Then he did the thing he'd never done in that Glasgow kitchen, because nobody there had ever shown him how.

He breathed out, slowly, once. And he turned around and said: "That wasn't about you. That was my wiring going off. You were right, I said seven. I'm sorry. Can I try that again?"

His partner looked at him for a moment, surprised. Then they moved over on the sofa.

The alarm didn't stop being fast. It probably never will. But for the first time, Callum had caught the gap between the reaction and the person, and in that gap there was room for a different choice.

That gap is where all of this starts.

---

Your wiring decides what fires. But some of what you do every day doesn't fire at all. It just *runs*, the way it has run a thousand times before.

That's the next layer. It's the paths you've worn.
