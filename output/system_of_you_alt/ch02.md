# Chapter 2: Body — Start at the Bottom

Six forty-five in the evening, and Maren's shift starts at eight.

She's standing at the kitchen counter buttering toast for her daughter, and the blur is back. That soft unfocus at the edge of things, as though the world has slipped one frame behind her eyes. She slept four hours today. Maybe four and a half. The rest of the afternoon went on the school run, a load of washing, a phone call to the doctor about her own back, and a sandwich she ate standing up.

Her daughter is at the table, swinging her legs, telling a long story about a girl in her class and a pencil case. Maren is nodding in the right places. She's not hearing a word.

"Mum. *Mum.* Did you hear me?"

"I *heard* you." It comes out hard. Too hard for a five-year-old and a pencil case.

Her daughter goes quiet. Maren puts the knife down, closes her eyes, and feels the guilt arrive.

And then the voice that always follows the guilt: *What kind of mother snaps at her child over nothing?*

Here's the truth Maren can't see from inside that kitchen. That wasn't a mother failing. That was a body that had nothing left to give, being asked for one thing more.

---

## The layer everything stands on

This is the **Body**: the physical machine you run on. Sleep. Food. Water. Movement. Pain. Illness. The hormones and chemistry humming underneath all of it.

We tend to think of the body as the container for the interesting parts of us: the thoughts, the feelings, the personality. It isn't. It's the *first* part. Everything you'll ever think, feel, decide or say runs on this machine. When it's well-resourced, you have range: patience, humour, the ability to hear a neutral comment as neutral. When it's low, the ceiling on everything above it drops.

The idea for this book came to me in a hospital, while I was undergoing tests and trying to make sense of the way my body challenges me. One of the first things I came to understand was this: like most people, I had treated my body as the thing that carried me around, when it was actually the thing everything else was built on.

The body isn't the background of your life. It's the foundation.

---

## Why the body comes first

Three things happen when the body runs low, and they explain most bad afternoons.

**Your energy runs out.** You have a finite tank. No amount of positive thinking tops it up. Holding a hard conversation takes energy. So does starting a new habit, being patient with a child, or staying kind in traffic. When the tank is low, the most expensive things go first, and patience is one of the most expensive things you own.

**Your attention gets physical.** A tired brain doesn't think less clearly in some vague way. It thinks less clearly in measurable ways. Sleep loss slows the parts of the brain that handle judgement, focus and self-control.^3^ That feeling of *I should know better* very often traces back to a body that couldn't do better in that moment, not a character that wouldn't.

**Your emotional range narrows.** When you're depleted, irritation turns into anger more easily, and calm turns into numbness. Short sleep even raises stress hormones the following evening.^4^ So the body that slept badly last night is often, quite literally, more on edge tonight.

This is why the rule of this book starts here. **You can't reliably fix a higher layer while a lower one is broken.** If you try to rewrite an old belief, build a new routine, or mend a relationship while you're running on four hours' sleep and no lunch, the effort collapses. Not because you're weak. Because the platform can't hold the weight.

The body isn't the only layer. But it's the first one to check, and it's the one most often overlooked.

---

## What it gets mistaken for

The single most common mistake people make with the body is treating a *physical* signal as a *moral* one.

- **"I'm lazy."** Often you're not lazy. You're running on empty, and pushing through just borrows against tomorrow at a high rate of interest.
- **"I'm a bad parent / partner / friend."** The snap, the missed message, the short temper: very often these are the body's limits showing, not a verdict on who you are.
- **"I'm anxious."** Sometimes. But too much coffee or a missed meal can produce the same racing heart and can't-settle feeling. The body can imitate worry very convincingly.
- **"I'm just low."** Flatness, fog and low drive can absolutely be about mood. They can also be about sleep, food and rest. If the heaviness lifts after a few properly fed, properly rested days, it was the body talking.
- **"I just need to try harder."** Effort doesn't refuel a tank. Trying harder on an empty body digs the hole deeper.

The body doesn't lie. It just runs low.

---

## What it's costing you: the three currencies

Here's the second question from the start of the book, and this is where it really comes alive: **what is it costing me?**

Your whole system runs on three things. You'll feel them in every layer, but the body is where they're easiest to notice, so this is the place to learn them.

**Fuel** is how much you have in the tank. When it's low, everything feels like effort: heavy limbs, foggy head, no drive. You feel *empty*.
*What restores it:* real sleep, real food, real rest. Not substitutes.

**Pressure** is what you're holding, and whether it's moving. When it builds, you feel it as weight and tightness: the clenched jaw, the shallow breath, the knot in the stomach, the shoulders up by your ears. You feel *held*.
*What restores it:* letting it out. Movement, a long breath, putting it into words.

**Charge** is how switched-on you are right now. Too high, and you're jittery, wired, can't sit still, everything feels urgent. Too low, and you're flat and switched off. You feel *buzzing*, or *gone*.
*What restores it:* settling the system. A slower breath, movement, a change of pace.

Here's the part most people miss. These can move independently. You can be high on Charge and empty on Fuel at the same time. That's the *wired-tired* feeling, buzzing on caffeine and adrenaline while the tank is completely dry. It's one of the most dangerous states to be in, because the buzz hides the emptiness until you crash.

Maren at the kitchen counter? Fuel almost empty. Pressure high, in her lower back and jaw. Charge spiking the moment her daughter raised her voice. Three readings, one tired woman.

---

## How it shows up

Low Fuel wears different clothes in different lives.

**Grace** skipped lunch again, because the client calls ran into each other and stopping to eat felt like an indulgence. At ten past three she's reading the same email for the fourth time and the words won't stick. She decides she's losing her edge. She's actually just hungry.

**Callum** pulled an all-nighter to finish his drawings and has been running on energy drinks since six. He feels sharp, fast, almost electric. That's Charge. Underneath it, his Fuel is at zero, and by five o'clock he'll be lying on the studio floor wondering why he can't think.

**Marcus** hasn't slept properly since the funeral. He wakes at four most mornings and lies there listening to the house. His appetite went somewhere and hasn't come back. He'd tell you he's fine. His body would tell you a different story, if anyone asked it.

Three different lives. The same underlying state: a body running low, quietly lowering the ceiling on everything above it.

---

## The tool for this layer: the Honest Refill

You'll meet all twelve tools in Chapter 8. But each layer chapter gives you one to start with, and for the Body it's the simplest and most often skipped.

**The Honest Refill** means giving your body the real thing it's short of, not the substitute.

1. **Name which fuel is lowest.** Sleep, food, water or rest. Be honest. It's usually the one you've been ignoring.
2. **Give the real thing.** Tired needs *sleep*, not more coffee. Hungry needs *food*, not a biscuit at your desk. Depleted needs *rest*, not a screen.
3. **Make it small enough to actually happen.** One earlier night. One proper meal, sitting down. One genuine hour off, without guilt.

That's it. It isn't glamorous. It's the single most underrated thing in this book.

---

## Try this: one honest change

**Notice.** How has your energy been over the last week? Not good or bad. Just: when did it dip, and when did it lift? See if one time of day stands out.

**Trace.** Think of a moment this week when you snapped, went foggy or couldn't be bothered. What was your body running on just then? Enough sleep, or not? Fed, or empty?

**Try.** Pick whichever is lowest: sleep, food or movement. Change one small thing tomorrow. Twenty minutes earlier to bed. A proper lunch. A ten-minute walk. Small enough that you'll actually do it.

**Watch.** The day after, notice what shifted. Were you steadier? Slower to snap? Nothing at all? There's no wrong answer. You're gathering what's true.

**Adjust.** If it helped, keep it and add nothing else yet. If it didn't, you've just ruled one thing out, which means the real cause is somewhere else. That's progress, not failure.

> *One line for the picture of yourself:*
> **"What my body needs most, and what I usually give it instead, is ______."**

---

## When it's more than tiredness

Sometimes you do all of this, honestly, for weeks, and the exhaustion doesn't lift. Or the pain doesn't ease. Or the fog stays no matter how much you sleep.

That's important information. It may mean something medical is going on: a condition, a deficiency, the side effect of a medication, pain that needs treating at its source. In that case the right next step isn't a better routine. It's a doctor.

That isn't the body layer failing you. It's the body layer doing its job, sending a signal loud enough to be heard. Listen to it.

---

## What Maren did next

Maren didn't fix her life that evening. She couldn't. The shift was still at eight, and the rota wasn't hers to change.

But she did one thing. She sat down. She made herself a proper plate, eggs and toast, and ate it at the table opposite her daughter instead of standing at the counter. And when she'd finished, she said: "I'm sorry I was sharp. I'm really tired. It wasn't about you. Tell me about the pencil case again."

The blur didn't vanish. But the evening softened. And on the drive to work, for the first time in months, Maren didn't spend the journey telling herself she was a terrible mother. She told herself something truer: *I was running on empty. That's a problem I can work on.*

That's the shift from blame to location. It's small. It changes everything.

---

The body is the foundation. But what about the times when you've slept, you've eaten, the tank is full, and you *still* react before you've had a chance to think?

That's the next layer up. It's the part of you that moves first.
